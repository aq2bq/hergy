# hergy-rules

A hergy team's rules are files: one team's agreements in `teams/<team>/agreement.yaml`, the agreement every team shares in `agreement.yaml`, and one file per role in `roles/<role>.yaml` (the last two under the project's `.hergy/` or, outside it, `~/.config/hergy/`). They are YAML, but the person you are working with does not write them. You ask, they answer, and you write the file.

This skill is for the person's side of that: writing a new team's rules, changing an existing team's rules, moving rules out of Markdown, and filling in the origins later. The shape of every key is in `hergy cheat --ai`; the format and the checks are what this skill walks through.

The rules can also be changed on the screen: `hergy serve` shows the role documents at `/roles` and a team's own agreement on the team's row at `/teams` (its チームの約束 tab). Both open a rule in place, keep it in the file but out of the prompt, reorder sections and rules, and show the Markdown each member reads. The agreement every team shares (`agreement.yaml`) is not on the screen: it is written as a file. Send the person to the screen for a change they want to make themselves; come back to this skill when the change needs the questions (a new team, a move out of Markdown, an origin to fill in, or the shared agreement).

## What you keep to

1. **The person writes no YAML and no long prose.** You ask one thing at a time, with the choices and your recommendation: for the text of a rule you offer a draft, and they choose or correct it. **The origin of a rule that is already there is never asked for from memory.** You build a candidate from the records — the git history and the gy ledger — and they answer yes, different, or I do not know, one rule at a time. The origin of a rule you are writing now is different: nobody has to remember anything, because the person is telling you why it is needed as they ask for it, and that answer is the rule's first record. You ask that one once, and it stops being asked about.
2. **You invent no origin, and no check.** A candidate you bring is a quotation from the records, never a story you assemble — and it has to be a record that speaks about *this* rule, not merely one whose words match (a commit that moved or shortened the file says where the text came from, not why the rule exists). An empty origin is honest where a made-up one is a lie the file will carry, and `hergy validate` then counts the rule as one nobody can account for, which is what it is. The person's yes is not a check on a candidate: they are being asked because they do not remember, so the record has to carry the meaning on its own. The same goes for the check: if nobody can say which report field or test shows the rule was kept, write neither.
3. **You check what you wrote before you put it down.** Run `hergy validate`, and show the person the prompt of each member with `hergy up <team> --dry`. A rule that no member reads, or a rule whose text lost a sentence, is a bug in the file.
4. **Only the person removes a rule.** Deciding that an old rule is dead, and deleting the Markdown once it has moved, are the person's calls. Ask; do not tidy up on your own. When the person wants to try a team without a rule, offer `in_prompt: false` first: the rule stays in the file, leaves every member's prompt, and comes back by deleting that one line. An origin that stays empty is not a duty: it marks a rule nobody can account for, which is a reason to look at that rule, not a reason to invent an origin for it.

5. **Put each sentence where it belongs.** A role document says how the work itself is done, and holds nothing that changes when the team, the tools or the model change. Who decides, who hands the turn to whom, who may not overrule whom, which tools the team uses (gy, git, CI, `hergy hire`) and this project's own facts (paths, sizes) go in the team's agreement (`teams/<team>/agreement.yaml`). Who may make which write to gy goes in `team.yaml` as `ledger:` (`req approve by lead`), next to `turns:`, and `hergy validate` checks it against the ledger. An instruction that only one harness can follow (a Claude Code skill to read, say) is not hergy's to hold: write it in the brief the lead sends, not in a rule. When one rule mixes these, split it.

## The four flows

### Write a new team

1. Write the starter files with `hergy new <team>` (inside a git repository it writes into the project's `.hergy/`; outside one, into `~/.config/hergy/`; start in the project). You get the team's `team.yaml` and `agreement.yaml`, and `roles/lead.yaml` and `roles/builder.yaml`.
2. Ask, one at a time: what is this team for; who are its members and what is each one's role; what does each role do that no other role does. Write the members and the roles into `team.yaml`, and what the team is for as its `mission:` — one line, near the top of the file.
3. For each rule, ask in this order:
   - the text: you draft one or two sentences from what they said, and read it back for a yes or a correction. When the rule hands the turn between two of the team — a member name, a role name, or the human — write it as `<name> → <name>`, or `<name> ⇄ <name>` when the turn goes both ways: `hergy validate` checks every such arrow against `turns:`. A rule like this usually belongs in the team's agreement, not in a role (item 5 of "What you keep to");
   - the origin: is this rule here because something went wrong (an incident, with its date), because someone asked for it (an instruction, whose and when), or because it follows from another rule (which id)? If the person does not know, write no origin: it can be filled in later, and `hergy validate` keeps counting the rule as one nobody can account for;
   - the reason (`why`): the sentence a member needs to judge a case the rule does not cover. Ask whether the members should read it: if not, keep it in `why` and set `send_why: false` — a reason that is not sent is still a reason, and `hergy validate` counts it. A `note` is something else: a memo for whoever maintains the file, never sent, and never counted as a reason;
   - the check: is there a report field that shows the rule was kept (write it under `reports`), a test, or neither? Neither is an answer.
4. Finish with item 3 of "What you keep to": `hergy validate`, then `hergy up <team> --dry` for the person.

### Fix an existing team

1. Run `hergy validate`. It ends with a count of what the rules carry and then lists, file by file, the id of every rule with no origin. Start from that list.
2. Fill in the origins with "Fill in the origins later": you build a candidate out of the records, and the person answers yes, different, or I do not know. Never ask them what they remember — the records answer first. A rule the records say nothing about is not asked about at all: at the end, tell the person how many are still empty. If they want to drop one of them, take those one at a time, following item 4 of "What you keep to".
3. Ask the same for the reason and for the check, in that order. Here the text is in front of you, so you are asking what the rule should say, not what happened once.
4. Finish with item 3 of "What you keep to".

### Move rules out of Markdown

Older teams keep their rules in Markdown (`protocol.md`, `roles/<role>.md`). hergy does not read Markdown any more: a `docs` entry that names a `.md` file, a directory that holds only `.md`, or a role that has only `<role>.md` stops with a message that says so. You move the rules by hand, with the person, in this order. Do the mechanical part yourself: everything the move needs is in the Markdown, so you finish the moving without asking, and the origins are left to "Fill in the origins later".

1. **Split the Markdown into units.** A unit is one of: a bulleted or numbered item; a table cell (a cell, not the whole row, and not the header row); a bold sentence inside a paragraph; or a paragraph with no bold. A heading and an HTML comment are not units: a heading becomes a `heading`, and a comment never reached the agents — the reason it holds is what step 3 moves. Count the units per file; that count is what you will check at the end.
2. **Group the units into rules and give each one an id.** Short, uppercase, per file: a prefix for the file and section, then a number (`TEAM-TURN-1`, `SPEC-AUD-5`). The order of the file is the order of the sections and of the rules inside them, as in the Markdown. A Markdown heading becomes a section `heading` (its depth, when the structure needs it, becomes `level`) — a heading is not a rule, and a rule is not a heading. A paragraph usually stays one rule even when only its bold sentence is the point: the bold sentence is the unit you will check at the end, not the boundary of the rule. A table becomes one rule per row, and its cells are separate units when you check at the end. If the text of a rule points at another rule by number ("rule 3", "the seven rules above"), rewrite it with the id. Watch for a rule that has grown a reason inside its own sentence: the reason becomes `why` and leaves the text. The members go on reading it, since they read it until now, so write no `send_why`.
3. **Move the origin out of the text.** A date, a case number or "on the instruction of ..." written inside the sentence becomes an `origin` entry: `kind: incident` with the date and a reference, or `kind: instruction`, or `kind: derived` with `refs`. Take it out of the text. A reason in an HTML comment becomes `why` with `send_why: false`, because comments never reached the agents and the reason was not sent. Together with step 2, that pair decides both cases without asking anyone.
4. **Take rules out of `intro`.** An `intro` is a paragraph before the rules and is not a rule. If an intro carries a rule ("communication is the handing over of the turn"), make it a rule of its own with an id, and leave the intro as the lead-in it was meant to be.
5. **Write the new files beside the old ones** — `<name>.yaml` next to `<name>.md` — so nothing is lost while you work. Then point `team.yaml`'s `docs` and `roles` at them: a directory in `docs` now means its `*.yaml` files, in name order, and a role is looked up as `<role>.yaml`. Write no `revision` unless the person asks for one: moving rules is not raising a version.
6. **Check what moved.** Run `hergy validate`, then take `hergy up <team> --dry --json`. For each member, its `sources` name the files it reads; every unit you counted in step 1 must appear in that member's `prompt`. Compare with the Markdown's marks dropped: `**`, backticks, the `|` of a table and whitespace, all gone. A table cell is checked on its own, because the row it was in is written as a list. Report the number of units that did not appear. Keep fixing until that number is 0. Two differences are not losses: a table becomes a list, and a line break may move.
7. **Leave the origins that are not written in the text empty.** Moving rules does not ask for origins: a date, a case number or "on the instruction of ..." written inside a sentence moves to `origin` (step 3), and nothing else does. The rules that end up with no origin stay that way, and "Fill in the origins later" can bring a candidate for them from the records — later, or not at all. Moving rules is a mechanical job, and a person asked to remember 80 origins will stop halfway.
8. **Ask about the old Markdown.** Once `hergy up <team> --dry` is clean and the person is satisfied, ask whether to delete the `.md` files. Deleting them is theirs to decide.

### Fill in the origins later

An origin that is empty is not a broken rule: the rule works, and the emptiness marks the one thing nobody can judge yet — whether the rule is still needed. Filling origins in is a job you do from the records, not from the person's memory. You bring a candidate; they say whether it is right.

1. Run `hergy validate` and take the rules it lists as having no origin. Work through them one at a time.
2. Build the candidate for one rule out of the records, not out of the conversation. Leave out the commits that say nothing about why the rule exists:
   - the commit that put the rule there: `git log -S "<a phrase from the rule's text>"` lists the commits that added or changed that phrase, newest first, so read them from the oldest. A commit whose message only moves, splits, merges or shortens the file (a rename, a bulk edit, "condense this", "288 lines to 155") is not material: it says where the text came from, not why the rule exists. Neither is a commit that turned Markdown into YAML;
   - what the rule came from is a record that says why it went in: a commit message, a pull request or issue body, or a decision, that says something went wrong, or that someone asked for this, about what the rule itself says. Take `kind` from the words of that record — do not infer it. If the records give a date and nothing else, build no candidate;
   - the ledger, when the project keeps one: `gy list --type decision --grep "<a word from the rule>"`, kept to decisions from before or on the same day as the commit that added the rule. The same word alone is not material: the decision has to speak about this rule.
   Write the candidate out in full: `kind` (`incident` or `instruction`; `derived` only when the text itself says which rule it came from), `date`, `refs`, and a one-line `note` that quotes what you found.
3. Show the one candidate and ask one question: is this right, different, or not known? "Different" means the person knows something the records do not — take their words and build the candidate again. "Not known" leaves the origin empty. One rule at a time; never the whole list.
4. A rule whose records say nothing gets no candidate and no question: leave it empty. Do not assemble a story to fill the gap (item 2 of "What you keep to"). An empty origin is a fact about the records, and it is the reason that rule shows up in the list again next time. At the end of the pass, tell the person how many rules are still empty; do not read them out one by one asking to keep or drop each.
5. Stop when the person stops. Origins left empty stay empty, and every rule is usable as it is.

## What good looks like at the end

- `hergy validate` exits 0, and its count says where each rule came from, or says plainly that nobody knows.
- `hergy up <team> --dry` shows each member a prompt that holds every rule that member used to read, with its reason where the reason belongs and without the maintenance notes.
- The origins that are filled in are quotations from the records; the rest are honestly empty, and each empty one names a rule to look at, not a gap to paper over.
- The person answered questions; they did not write YAML.
